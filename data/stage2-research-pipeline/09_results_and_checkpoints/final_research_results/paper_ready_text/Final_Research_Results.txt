
FINAL RESEARCH RESULTS
======================

1. Label-Scarcity Performance
-----------------------------

The fully supervised Random Forest achieved a mean held-out
F1-score of 0.573 when trained using 100% of the
available training labels.

Under severe label scarcity, supervised performance declined.
With only 10% of the labels available, the supervised Random
Forest obtained an F1-score of 0.503.

Conventional pseudo-labeling increased the 10%-label F1-score
to 0.571, corresponding to an absolute improvement
of 0.069.

Negative-only pseudo-labeling achieved a similar F1-score of
0.571.

At 20% labeled data, supervised F1 was 0.541,
compared with 0.571 for conventional pseudo-labeling
and 0.571 for negative-only pseudo-labeling.

At 50% labeled data, the performance difference narrowed:
supervised F1=0.561,
conventional pseudo-labeling F1=0.570,
and negative-only pseudo-labeling F1=0.571.


2. Asymmetric Pseudo-Label Reliability
--------------------------------------

The pseudo-label diagnostics demonstrated that the selected
pseudo-label pool was overwhelmingly composed of high-confidence
negative examples.

The negative-only pseudo-labeling ablation produced performance
that was nearly indistinguishable from conventional pseudo-labeling
across the principal scarcity conditions.

This suggests that much of the semi-supervised improvement is
associated with the reliable expansion of the negative class
rather than balanced pseudo-label expansion across both classes.


3. Statistical Evidence
-----------------------

Twenty frozen-seed repetitions confirmed that pseudo-labeling
produced the strongest improvements when labeled data were scarce.

At the 10% and 20% label conditions, both conventional and
negative-only pseudo-labeling demonstrated consistent paired
F1 improvements over the supervised Random Forest.

The effect decreased considerably as labeled-data availability
increased toward 50%.


4. Explainability
-----------------

Two complementary post-hoc explainability methods were used:
grouped permutation importance and SHAP.

The methods produced a Spearman feature-rank correlation of
approximately 0.797, indicating strong agreement in the overall
feature ranking.

The consensus top predictors were:

1. Gender
2. Item Purchased
3. Location

Gender was the dominant predictor according to both explanation
methods.


5. Gender Structural Analysis
-----------------------------

A direct Gender × Subscription Status analysis identified a
pronounced dataset-level association.

Cramer's V was 0.417.

A post-hoc Gender-ablation sensitivity analysis further showed
that removing Gender reduced mean F1 from 0.573
to 0.281, an absolute decrease of
0.291 and a relative decrease of approximately
50.9%.

ROC-AUC decreased from approximately 0.719
to 0.491, indicating that discrimination was
near chance when Gender was unavailable.


6. Scientific Interpretation
----------------------------

The results support the use of pseudo-labeling for recovering
classification performance under label scarcity in this dataset.

However, the explainability and robustness analyses show that
the predictive problem contains a strong structural dependency
between Gender and Subscription Status.

Because the Customer Shopping Trends dataset is synthetic,
this relationship must be interpreted as a characteristic of
the dataset rather than a causal or automatically generalizable
real-world behavioral effect.

The primary research contribution is therefore twofold:

(1) demonstrating data-efficient subscription prediction under
label scarcity through pseudo-labeling; and

(2) revealing, through explainability and ablation analysis,
that apparently strong predictive performance may be heavily
dependent on structural properties of the synthetic dataset.
